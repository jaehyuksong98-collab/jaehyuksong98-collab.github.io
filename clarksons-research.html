Oil prices and Broking revenue

I tested the idea that either a rise or a fall in oil prices must help Broking revenue. I compared oil prices, freight indicators and reported revenue, then considered how chartering, ship sales and newbuilding activity differ.

How it fits: Helped me avoid using the oil price as a direct revenue driver in the scenario model.

Reading note: This is a directional check across three comparisons, with a freight proxy in one comparison. It cannot prove a common cause or rule out a demand channel. The PDF’s stronger causal wording should be read with that limit.

Oil price outlook

I gathered dated oil-price snapshots and forecasts from several institutions, compared the assumptions behind them, and flagged a source conflict around the OCBC attribution.

How it fits: Background for the model’s disruption scenarios and the questions I need to keep following.

Reading note: Forecast horizons differ: year-end targets, quarterly averages and annual averages are not directly comparable. The PDF sometimes groups them together. Prices are a dated snapshot, and the September OCBC claim remains unverified.

Company news and market log

I collected company announcements and market reports between earnings releases, and noted which items could change an assumption and which were only useful background.

How it fits: A record to return to when reviewing acquisitions, new services, freight conditions and later results.

Reading note: This predates v5.3. Its M&A assumptions and some market commentary refer to an earlier model. Use the final workbook for current project assumptions; aggregator price targets are not primary research.

Operating profit structure workbook

I assembled segment revenue and operating profit, then calculated revenue mix, margins, head-office cost absorption, geographic mix and per-employee measures.

How it fits: The source table behind my segment comparisons and the starting point for choosing different assumptions by business line.

Reading note: Segment profit is shown before head-office costs. The workbook’s “Group Average” operating profit per employee uses the segment total before head office; it should not be read as consolidated group profit per employee.

How the business earns revenue

I mapped the four segments to their revenue models and the work people do in each area, using Clarksons’ business-model and careers pages.

How it fits: Helped me connect the financial statements to commissions, financing fees, port services and recurring research income.

Reading note: This is qualitative website research. It is not an audited disclosure or evidence of a specific customer contract.

Margins, compensation and the balance sheet

I compared changes in revenue and implied costs, separated bonus expense from ESOP cash purchases, and examined cash, receivables and short-term settlement balances.

How it fits: Informed the model’s flow-through margins, working-capital treatment and distinction between profit and cash.

Reading note: The interim receivables line includes trade and other receivables. The ex-settlement DSO is a proxy, not a pure trade-debtor measure. Its complement does not establish the share of revenue collected in cash. Bonus timing alone also cannot establish earnings quality.
