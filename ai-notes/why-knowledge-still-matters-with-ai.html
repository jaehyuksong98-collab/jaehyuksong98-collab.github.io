Attitudes & learning / AI & judgment

When AI can give me an answer, why should I keep learning?

Access to an answer is not the same as understanding it. My interest in AI and automation makes me think more carefully about knowledge, value and responsibility.

2026-10-13BY JAEHYUK SONG

The question behind the convenience

I often hear the argument that knowledge becomes less valuable as AI becomes more capable. If anyone can ask for an explanation, generate a report or receive an expert-sounding answer, why spend years learning to do those things? As a BBA student studying finance and interested in automation, I take that question seriously.

I expect the shape of professional work to change. Some tasks may be replaced, and some kinds of expertise may command less of a premium. But I hesitate when that becomes a reason to stop learning. It seems to skip a more fundamental question: what was the work supposed to achieve in the first place?

An answer does not become my knowledge automatically

I would not reduce the value of accountants or consultants to knowing things their clients do not. Their work can also involve interpretation, implementation, trust and accountability. Still, access to specialist information has been one part of the gap between a professional and a client. AI makes me question how much value can continue to come from simply supplying that information.

What it does not do automatically is turn the answer into the reader’s understanding. I can receive an explanation without knowing when it applies, what it leaves out or how to recognise a mistake.

In finance, that distinction feels especially important. An AI-generated model would not, by itself, mean I understood the company. Seeing that revenue increased is different from judging whether that growth can continue. A formula can be correct while the assumption behind it is unreasonable. Being able to produce a model and being able to explain why I trust it are different capabilities.

What value am I actually creating?

My interest in AI and n8n comes from wanting to make financial data processing more efficient. I would like to spend less effort moving and organising information, and more effort understanding what it means. But speed alone would be a poor measure of whether I had done useful work.

If I define the problem badly, automation could help me produce the wrong result more efficiently. A polished report or an elaborate workflow can look like progress while leaving the original question unanswered.

Before deciding what to automate, I want to be able to explain the goal, who benefits and what would count as a useful result. That requires knowledge of the work itself. Knowing how to operate a tool is only one part of knowing how to use it well.

The responsibility behind my name

This is where the question becomes ethical for me. If I pass on an analysis I do not understand, what am I asking another person to trust? They may reasonably read a clear explanation and precise numbers as signs that I have checked the work. The presentation could communicate more confidence than I have earned.

Saying that I used AI would provide context, but it would not answer whether the analysis was sound. If my name is on the work, I should be able to explain the sources, the assumptions I accepted and the uncertainty that remains. Admitting what I do not understand is part of that responsibility, especially when someone else’s money or work could be affected.

I also do not think responsibility should fall entirely on the individual. Organisations that encourage AI use should provide time and clear standards for review. Demanding more output while leaving people without the means to check it would make responsible use much harder.

Why I still want to learn

I see real promise in making explanations and learning support more accessible. But access needs to be accompanied by the ability to evaluate what we receive. Otherwise, I worry that a gap in access to information could become a gap between people who can judge an answer and people who have to take it on trust.

I would not claim that every kind of knowledge will retain its current market value. Recalling and relaying information may become less scarce. Yet that does not remove my reason to study. I need a foundation of my own to question an answer, connect it to a business problem and recognise when I need help.

The capability I want to develop is being able to use AI while still explaining, in my own words, why I accepted the result. As answers become easier to produce, I want to become more deliberate about which ones I am willing to stand behind.
